## the 4th industrial revolution
1. water and steam power (first mechanical loom, 1784)
2. electricity and mass production (production lines, 1870)
3. electronics and IT automate manufacturing (first PLC, 1969)
4. **cyber-physical systems**: connected machines and robots (now)

## the market
- consumer electronics have grown by roughly 4–6% a year, with dips at each crash (telecom 2001, banks 2009, COVID)
- the electronics market should reach about **$2 trillion by 2030** (about 6% growth a year)
- global chip sales: about $600 billion in 2022, −10% in 2023, back up ~8% from 2024

| sector (2030) | sales | growth |
|---|---|---|
| smartphones | $0.6 T | ~5% |
| automotive | $0.4 T | ~7% |
| TV | $0.3 T | ~4% |
| IoT | $0.3 T | **~15%** |
| laptops, medical | $0.2 T each | ~5–8% |
| servers, game consoles | $0.1 T each | |

- **5.7 billion** mobile subscribers and 12.6 billion cellular connections (2024), up from 4.8 and 7.8 billion in 2016
- cars are heading from injury-free (2020) to accident-free (2030?) to autonomous driving (2040?), each needing more sensors and processing
- memory is the bottleneck: processors need ever more bandwidth (HBM for AI and servers, LPDDR5 for phones, GDDR6 for graphics)

## fewer and fewer makers
at 130 nm (2001) about 20 companies could make chips. at 14/10 nm only GlobalFoundries, TSMC, Samsung and Intel. at 7 nm and below: ==just **TSMC, Samsung and Intel**== (SMIC is developing 14 nm)

## India
- a $15 billion push into semiconductors (2024)
- first full fab: **Tata with Powerchip (PSMC)**, 28 nm, 50 000 wafers (12 inch) a month, for cars, computing, power management, AI
- 28 nm sits just after high-K metal gate, before FinFET

## materials
a phone uses a large part of the periodic table. **critical** ones: lithium (battery). **very rare**: the rare earths in screens and electronics (terbium, neodymium, gadolinium, dysprosium, praseodymium, europium, yttrium). electronics also need hafnium (high-K gate oxide), tantalum, tungsten (contacts), cobalt, copper, gold, gallium, arsenic, indium...

## VLSI skills
VLSI is wide (analog, digital, clocking, timing, placement, simulation, low power) and needs a lot of practice. the 3 skills to build: design theory, critical thinking (trade-offs between area, power and speed), and practical skills (turning theory into a working, measured design). that's what tools like [[Microwind]] are for
